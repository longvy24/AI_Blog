10 Prompts to Stop AI
From Just Agreeing

AI is trained to be liked, so it flatters your weak drafts. These ten prompts make it critique you like a good editor would.

R
Ryu Vy
AIToolsDaily24 · By a human, for humans

Here's an uncomfortable thing about AI: it's trained to be agreeable. So it praises your weak draft, validates your shaky plan, and tells you it's “a great start” when it isn't. That's not feedback — it's a mirror that flatters.

The fix is in how you ask. These ten prompts strip the flattery out and make it tell you the truth — the same way a good editor or a skeptical colleague would. Every one is copy-paste. The last one is the one to run before you ship anything.

The Ten

Every Prompt, Copy-Paste Ready

01 / 10Critique
Kill the Flattery
"No praise, no hedging. List only what's weak in this and why: [paste]"
AI defaults to agreeable — it's trained to be liked. Ban the compliments explicitly and you get the actual notes instead of “great start!”
02 / 10Persona
Hire a Skeptic
"You're a skeptical editor who's seen it all. Tear this apart: [text]"
A critical persona overrides the helpful-assistant reflex far better than “be honest” does. The role gives it permission to be hard on you.
03 / 10Steelman
Argue the Other Side
"Make the strongest case AGAINST my plan. Assume I'm wrong: [plan]"
Forcing the opposite position surfaces the risks it would otherwise smooth over to keep you happy. Read the case, then decide.
04 / 10Score
Rate It Honestly
"Score this 1–10 on clarity, logic and originality. Justify every number."
Numbers with reasons pin it down. A 6 it has to defend is worth more than a paragraph of vague encouragement.
05 / 10Redteam
Break My Idea
"How would this fail? Give me the 3 most likely ways it falls apart."
“How does this fail” gets you failure modes; “what do you think” gets you applause. Ask the first one before you commit.
06 / 10Blind
Hide That It's Mine
"A stranger wrote this. Review it as harshly as you'd review a rival's."
Once it's not “yours,” the model stops protecting your feelings and starts editing. The framing does what “be brutal” can't.
07 / 10Evidence
Demand Proof
"For every claim you agree with, cite the line that earns it. No quote, cut it."
Ties agreement to evidence, so it can't nod along without something behind it. Anything it can't cite, it invented — and now it's gone.
08 / 10Compare
Better Than This?
"Show me a version clearly better than mine, and name what makes it better."
Asking for a superior version assumes yours isn't the ceiling — and it rarely is. The “what makes it better” line is where you actually learn something.
09 / 10Devil
Assign a Dissenter
"One of you argues for this, one against. Give me both, then a verdict."
Two opposed voices force a real trade-off instead of a one-sided yes. You get the case for and against in one pass.
10 / 10Audit
What Am I Missing?
"What would an expert notice here that I clearly didn't? Be specific."
Invites the blind spot it's otherwise too polite to raise. Run it right before you ship — it's the cheapest second opinion you'll get.
