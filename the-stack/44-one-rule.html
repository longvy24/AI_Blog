One Rule.
Never Feed It Company Data.

The entire AI policy where I work is a single sentence: use whatever tool you like, just never put company data into it. Easy to say. Here's how you actually hold that line day to day — and the weekly self-audit that tells you the moment you've slipped.

The 22-second version · Stage 1 of 5

This is where the series starts, so it starts with the rule. One sentence, and it was the whole AI policy where I work: use anything you like — never put company data in it. No approved-vendor list, no blocked sites, no training module. Just the one line, and the assumption that adults could hold it.

The trouble is that the rule is easy to say and slippery to keep. "Company data" sounds obvious until you're mid-task at 4pm, pasting in a spreadsheet to get a formula fixed, and the spreadsheet happens to have real client names down column A. Nobody decides to leak. You leak by convenience, one harmless-looking paste at a time.

The entire policy
Use anything you like.
Never put company data in it.

So the skill isn't willpower. It's a set of habits that let you get the model's help on the real problem without ever handing it the real data. Once I'd built them they stopped feeling like caution and started feeling like the normal way to ask.

The Move

Describe The Shape, Not The Specifics

Almost every real task can be split into its shape and its specifics. The shape is the structure of the problem: the columns, the relationships, the logic you need help with. The specifics are the actual values — the names, the numbers, the identifiers that make it yours. The model almost never needs the specifics to solve the shape.

So you hand it the shape and withhold the specifics. Keep the real schema, swap in fake rows. Keep the structure of the document, replace the contents with placeholders. You get an answer that fits your problem exactly, and nothing that could identify anyone ever left the building.

01 / 04Abstract
Describe The Shape
"Here's the structure of my problem, no real data: [columns / logic / goal]. Solve for the structure."
Lead with the shape and you'll find the model needs the specifics far less often than you assumed. The structure is the part it's actually good at. The values are the part only you should hold.
02 / 04Substitute
Fake Rows, Real Schema
"Same columns and types as my file, but invent the rows. Write the query against those."
The schema is what the logic hangs on, so keep it exact. The rows are what identify people, so make them up. A query written against a faithful schema and invented data drops onto the real file unchanged — and the real file never moved.
03 / 04Redact
Placeholder Names
"Use CLIENT_A, VENDOR_B, PROJECT_X in place of anything real. Keep them consistent."
You still need the relationships — that A pays B, that X depends on Y — but you never need the identities. Consistent placeholders preserve the wiring and strip the names. Map them back yourself, offline, in ten seconds.
04 / 04Audit
The Weekly Leak Test
"Based only on what I've pasted this week, what could you guess about my employer? Name the industry if you can."
This is the one almost nobody runs. Turn the model back on yourself and ask what it's pieced together. If it can name your industry, your region, or your company from the fragments you've fed it, you've been leaking in slices too thin to notice. A clean answer means your habits are holding.
Why It Matters

Leaks Are Cumulative

The reason the self-audit is the important habit — not the flashy one, the important one — is that leaks don't happen in a single dramatic paste. They accumulate. One session you give it a real table name. Another, a project codename. Another, a revenue figure to sanity-check. Each one is harmless alone. Stacked up over a month, they draw a recognisable picture of exactly where you work.

No single paste trips your own alarm, which is precisely why you need a habit that looks at the pile. Asking the model what it could infer is the cheapest audit you'll ever run, and the only one that catches slow leaks.

// The honest version

None of this was my policy to write and I'm not going to pretend the one rule was generous or stingy — it just was the rule, and it turned out to be a good teacher. Learning to get real help on a problem without handing over the specifics is a skill that outlives any one job or any one tool. The company set the line. The habits for staying inside it came out of holding it every day.
