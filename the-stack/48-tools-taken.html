They Took The Tools.

The 22-second version · Stage 3 of 5

Stage three arrived without warning. Where I worked, the policy changed overnight — external AI platforms were out, and one internal tool was in. No migration window, no transition plan. One day the tools I'd built a whole workflow around were simply gone.

The uncomfortable discovery came within a week: a lot of what I'd been calling "prompt skill" wasn't skill at all. It was muscle memory for one specific model — its quirks, its defaults, the things it would infer so I never had to say them. Move to a different model and half of that "skill" evaporates on contact.

The Discovery

It Was Muscle Memory, Not Skill

Real prompt skill is portable: stating a problem clearly, giving a clean example, naming a constraint out loud. Model-specific habits aren't: leaning on one model's willingness to infer, its house formatting, the shorthand it happened to understand. When the tool changes, the portable part comes with you and the habits don't. Telling the two apart, fast, is the whole game.

01 / 04Neutral
Write Model-Agnostic
"Assume no memory of me and no house style. Spell out format, tone, and limits."
A prompt that only works because one model quietly fills the gaps isn't a prompt, it's a dependency. Write so any competent model could execute it, and a switch can never strand you again.
02 / 04State
Say The Quiet Constraints
"Constraints I usually leave unsaid: [list them]. Apply every one."
Every model you've used has been silently honoring rules you stopped typing months ago. A new one knows none of them. Write down the constraints you'd forgotten you were even relying on — that's most of the gap.
03 / 04Carry
Keep Your Examples
"Here's a known-good example of what I want. Match this."
Examples travel where habits don't. A good input/output pair works on any model on day one — it's the most portable asset you own. Keep a folder of them; they outlive every tool you'll ever be handed.
04 / 04Own
Prompts In A File You Hold
"[Paste your standing prompts from a file you keep, not the tool.]"
The one I wish I'd done first. When the tool goes, its history goes with it — every prompt you refined inside it, gone with the login. Keep your prompts in a file you own, not in a tool you might lose access to overnight.
// The honest version

Losing the tools with no notice was the best portability lesson I got, and I'd never have volunteered for it. It forced me to separate what I actually knew from what one model had quietly been doing for me. None of it was my call — but the habit that came out of it, owning your prompts instead of renting them, is the one I'd hand anyone whose stack isn't fully theirs to control.
